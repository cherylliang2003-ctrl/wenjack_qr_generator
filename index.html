PARLIAMENT HILL LAWN
Centennial Flame
We stand at the Centennial Flame, a monument built to celebrate Canada's Confederation. While it symbolizes the political joining of provinces and territories — represented by the 13 shields surrounding the water — it also serves as a vital reminder of what was left out of that original foundation. As we walk for Chanie Wenjack and the thousands of Indigenous children impacted by the residential school system, this flame reminds us that true unity cannot exist without truth, justice, and the recognition of Indigenous sovereignty. Let this fire light our collective responsibility to dismantle systemic harms and build a future rooted in genuine reconciliation and respect for the original caretakers of this land.
Southeast via Laurier Ave to Rideau Canal
2
RIDEAU CANAL
Rideau Canal Locks
The Rideau Canal has connected communities for generations and reminds us of the waterways that sustained Indigenous peoples long before Confederation. As we conclude our Walk for Wenjack, this gathering place offers an opportunity for reflection, learning, and commitment. Reconciliation is not a destination but an ongoing journey that requires listening, action, and meaningful relationships with Indigenous communities.
West along Queen St to Confederation Park
3
CONFEDERATION PARK
Kwakiutl Totem Pole
This Kwakiutl Totem Pole, carved by master artist Henry Hunt, was gifted by British Columbia in 1971 to mark a milestone in Canada's political history. But as we stand before it today on our Walk for Wenjack, we see it through a much deeper lens. For generations, the Canadian government banned potlatches and actively tried to suppress Indigenous languages, spiritual traditions, and the carving of totem poles. Standing tall here in the heart of the nation's capital, this pole is a monumental symbol of cultural survival, resilience, and reclamation. It reminds us that despite every attempt by the residential school system to erase Indigenous identity, the culture, stories, and spirit of Indigenous communities remain unbroken, vibrant, and foundational to this land.
South on Elgin St to Confederation Square
4
CONFEDERATION SQUARE
National Aboriginal Veterans Monument
This monument honors the thousands of First Nations, Métis, and Inuit people who volunteered to serve in Canada's armed forces, standing up for freedom abroad while experiencing systemic injustice at home. Designed by Indigenous artist Lloyd Pinay, it represents unity and the deep connection between culture and sacrifice. The four warriors face the cardinal directions, balancing weapons with symbols of peace like the eagle feather. Surrounding them are four spiritual guide animals — the wolf, bear, buffalo, and caribou — representing family, healing, strength, and adaptability. At the peak sits the eagle, symbolizing the Creator and guiding spirit of Indigenous peoples.
